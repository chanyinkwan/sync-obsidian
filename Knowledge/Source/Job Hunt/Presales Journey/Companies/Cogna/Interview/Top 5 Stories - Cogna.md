## The five stories

### Story 1 — Consultative discovery and scoping

**Use for:** discovery, requirements, ambiguity, customer needs, scoping, tailoring.

> At Huawei I cover presales for a Tier-1 UK mobile operator, and the pursuit team needed a solid technical scope before we committed anything to the customer. My job was to make sure that scope was real rather than assumed.
>
> I ran **three** technical workshops and one structured requirements session with their engineering stakeholders. I mapped their objectives and their current environment first, then separated the genuine must-haves from the preferences. After that I tested the solution against European regulatory constraints across **six** of the account's subnets, and I wrote up the scope, the risks, the owners and the next steps for our **five-person** pursuit team.
>
> The bid went out built on requirements the customer had confirmed themselves, and we stopped carrying the risk of promising something nobody had validated.
>
> At Cogna, discovery is literally the revenue gate — you only invoice when the app proves value, so a scope the customer's own engineers have confirmed is what turns a Discover into a Deliver you can bill for.

**Anchors: three workshops, six subnets, validated scope.**

### Story 2 — Technical translation

**Use for:** explaining complexity, non-technical audience, demo, training, adoption.

> At Bank of China, teams were losing about **eight hours a week** assembling reports by hand across **20 systems**. I replaced that with a single Grafana observability view. Building it was the easy part. It was only worth anything if the system owners and the department heads actually used it.
>
> So in the demo I opened with the outcome they wanted, one live view instead of manual reporting, and only then showed how the data underneath connected. I pitched the depth differently for the **30-plus** system leads than for the two department heads. And I took their technical objections live in the room rather than promising to come back with answers.
>
> They adopted it in that session. It gave back roughly **400 hours a year**.
>
> That is the Cogna training-and-adoption problem exactly: your revenue only lands when a gas network's planners actually use the app and the saved hours show up, so I sell the workflow first and the architecture only as far as it makes the workflow believable.

**Anchors: 20 systems, 30-plus stakeholders, 400 hours returned.**

### Story 3 — Objection handling and stakeholder buy-in

**Use for:** disagreement, pushback, influence without authority, changing a decision.

> At Apple, headquarters had decided to support a POS migration with one centralised training approach. I thought it would fail, because the stores genuinely ran differently by location. I had no authority over that decision and no interest in arguing about preferences.
>
> So I gathered evidence on how store processes actually varied, translated those differences into the operational risk they would create, and put the case to the Shanghai development team. I did not just object either. I brought an alternative: keep the same migration objective, deliver the training store by store.
>
> They reversed the original plan and moved to localised delivery.
>
> Cogna's buyers are utilities with decades-old ways of working — getting an operations team to change how permits or work orders move takes exactly this: respect their goal, make the hidden constraint visible with their own evidence, and offer a route that still gets them there. No adoption, no proven value, no invoice.

**Anchors: central plan, store evidence, decision reversed.**

### Story 4 — Commercial judgment and value selling

**Use for:** commercial value, qualification, ROI, expansion, spotting opportunities.

> At Apple, a Hong Kong broadcaster walked in with no formal enterprise opportunity attached to them. I suspected the request in front of me was a symptom of something bigger, so I started asking how they chose, bought and managed their devices.
>
> I earned the right to keep asking by giving them real hands-on technical support and building them a custom pricing portal, then qualified the operational and commercial requirements over several conversations. When they raised worries about controlling a larger device fleet, I widened the conversation from hardware purchasing to device management instead of treating it as somebody else's problem.
>
> It became a **£50,000** deal over **three months**, and the same approach across a **50-plus** account portfolio brought in **£350,000** of new B2B pipeline.
>
> The JD says Analysts seek out new opportunities at existing customers — that is Cogna's growth model: land at a Cadent or an OCU with one app, then find the next operational problem worth an invoice. Qualifying on the business problem, not the shopping list, is where that expansion revenue comes from.

**Anchors: casual visit, £50,000 deal, £350,000 pipeline.**

### Story 5 — Problem-solving under pressure and fast learning

**Use for:** challenge, deadline, pressure, regulation, learning fast, coordination.

> At Bank of China, the HKMA issued a mandate that meant shipping Geo-IP fraud detection with impossible-travel logic in **14 days**. I had not worked that regulation before, and I owned turning the legal language into something internal teams and a third-party vendor could actually build and prove.
>
> I broke the mandate into functional, data, testing and approval requirements, pinned an owner and a dependency to each one, and held every decision against the evidence the regulator would ask for. That is what stopped the scope growing. Then I got the technical teams and the vendor working to one set of acceptance criteria instead of three.
>
> We shipped inside the deadline and met **100 percent** of the mandates.
>
> Your buyers are being pushed by Ofgem RIIO-3 licence conditions and the AMP8 investment period, not by a taste for AI — turning a regulatory obligation into an executable, evidenced delivery is precisely what they are buying from Cogna, and it is the environment I have already delivered in.

**Anchors: HKMA, 14 days, 100 percent delivered.**
