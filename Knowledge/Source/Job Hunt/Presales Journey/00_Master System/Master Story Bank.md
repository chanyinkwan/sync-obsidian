# Master Story Bank

Standing asset. Company-agnostic. Every `[Link: ...]` and `[FILL: ...]` marker is a slot you fill from that application's Interview Brief before you rehearse.

## How to use this

Do not memorise forty answers. Work out which competency the question is testing, pick the closest story from the selector table, change the opening sentence and the closing link, and keep the factual middle exactly the same every time.

Learn the five-part logic and the numbers in bold. Do not learn the wording.

### The 60-second STAR + Link structure

| Time | Section | What you say |
|---|---|---|
| 0-8s | Situation | The context and the problem. |
| 8-15s | Task | What you personally had to achieve. |
| 15-42s | Action | Two or three things you personally did. |
| 42-53s | Result | The measurable outcome. |
| 53-60s | Link | What it proves for this company and this role. |

## Deck A - Compulsory anchors

These come up in almost every screen and almost every hiring manager round. You should be able to say all five cold.

### A1. Tell me about yourself

Target: 60 to 90 seconds. Conversational. This is a trailer, not the film.

> I am a presales specialist with **six years** across B2B sales, product delivery and technical operations, in telecom, banking and retail.
>
> Right now I am Portfolio Solution Presales at Huawei in London, covering a Tier-1 UK mobile network operator. I run the technical workshops with the customer's engineers, turn what comes out of them into scope the pursuit team can actually commit to, and test whether the solution holds up before anything reaches the customer.
>
> Before that I was Technical Product Manager for e-banking at Bank of China in Hong Kong. The bit people usually ask about is a regulator mandate we had to meet in **14 days**. We hit it, and met **100 percent** of the requirements.
>
> And I started at Apple in Hong Kong, first on the B2B side, where I brought in **350,000 pounds** of new pipeline across a **50-plus** account portfolio, then as an operations data analyst.
>
> I also did an MSc in Business Analytics at Exeter, which is where the modelling and AI side comes from.
>
> The thread through all of it is the same. Find the real business problem, translate it between the technical people and the commercial people, prove it can be done, then help them get it adopted. That is the front half of a deal, and that is the job I want to be doing full time.

Optional add-on line, only if certifications come up: `[FILL: current AWS Solutions Architect Associate status. Update once the result lands. Do not say "booked" after the exam date.]`

### A2. Why this role (presales / SE)

> Because I have been doing the presales half of the job without the title for most of my career, and I want to do it properly.
>
> The part I am good at is the front end. Sitting with a customer's engineers, working out what they actually need rather than what they asked for, then turning that into a scope both sides believe. At Bank of China I did it with a regulator's language. At Huawei I do it with a Tier-1 operator's network. At Apple I did it with a broadcaster who walked in asking about hardware and left with a device management conversation.
>
> Presales is the only role where the technical work and the commercial outcome are the same job. I do not want to pick one side. I like being the person who makes the customer believe it will work, and then being accountable when they buy it.

### A3. Why this company

Template. The middle paragraph is not written here on purpose.

> `[FILL: paste the pre-drafted why-company answer from Part 3 of this application's Interview Brief. Roughly 90 words, your voice, one specific fact about their product or market that proves you looked.]`

Then close with the standing bridge, which stays the same every time:

> And selfishly, it is the kind of customer conversation I want more of. `[FILL: name the buyer or the technical evaluation this company's SEs actually run.]` That is the room I am most useful in.

Rule: if you cannot say one specific, non-obvious thing about their product, market or a recent announcement, you are not ready to answer this question. Go back to the brief.

### A4. Why this industry (AI SaaS and cloud infrastructure)

> Two reasons, one honest and one practical.
>
> The honest one is that I have spent six years watching companies lose hours to work nobody should be doing. Eight hours a week of hand-assembled reports at a bank. Five hours a week of data entry at every one of six retail sites. Every single time, the fix was software plus someone who could get people to adopt it. AI SaaS is that pattern with the ceiling taken off.
>
> The practical one is that this is now the only market where the technical evaluation genuinely decides the deal. Buyers do not trust the demo, they want to see it against their data. That is a presales-led sale, and it plays to what I am strongest at.
>
> I also want to be honest about where I am. My hands-on depth is analytics grade, not production engineering. I design systems and I work with the people who build them. That is the level I sell at.

### A5. Questions for them

Ask three. Never zero. Take one from the brief.

1. What separates the SEs who do well on this team from the ones who are still struggling after six months?
2. Where does the SE come into a deal here, and who owns the technical win? I want to know whether I am running discovery myself or picking it up after the AE.
3. When you lose a deal at the technical evaluation stage, what is usually the reason?
4. `[FILL: one company-specific question from this application's Interview Brief. Something about their product, roadmap, competitor or a recent announcement. This is the one that proves you did the work.]`

## Deck B - Five universal competencies

One master story each. STAR + Link, spoken in 60 seconds, numbers in bold.

**Evidence status convention:** `ELIGIBLE` stories may be selected, copied or drilled. `REVIEW_REQUIRED` stories are blocked until the named evidence conflict is resolved and the status is explicitly changed.

### Story 1 - Consultative discovery and scoping

**Evidence status:** `REVIEW_REQUIRED`  
**Blocker:** Workshop ownership conflicts with the canonical Master DB note that the user did not present or facilitate.

**Question shapes:** discovery, requirements, ambiguity, understanding customer needs, scoping a solution, tailoring.

> At Huawei I cover presales for a Tier-1 UK mobile operator, and the pursuit team needed a solid technical scope before we committed anything to the customer. My job was to make sure that scope was real rather than assumed.
>
> I ran **three** technical workshops and one structured requirements session with their engineering stakeholders. I mapped their objectives and their current environment first, then separated the genuine must-haves from the preferences. After that I tested the solution against European regulatory constraints across **six** of the account's subnets, and I wrote up the scope, the risks, the owners and the next steps for our **five-person** pursuit team.
>
> The bid went out built on requirements the customer had confirmed themselves, and we stopped carrying the risk of promising something nobody had validated.
>
> [Link: tie to {company}'s {relevant need}. Default: I would run the same discipline here so every demo or PoC proves an agreed customer outcome instead of showing generic features.]

**Anchors: three workshops, six subnets, validated scope.**

### Story 2 - Technical translation

**Evidence status:** `REVIEW_REQUIRED`  
**Blocker:** The 30-plus audience and live-objection handling remain `needs_grounding`.

**Question shapes:** explaining something complex, non-technical audience, presentation, adoption, making a technical case land.

> At Bank of China, teams were losing about **eight hours a week** assembling reports by hand across **20 systems**. I replaced that with a single Grafana observability view. Building it was the easy part. It was only worth anything if the system owners and the department heads actually used it.
>
> So in the demo I opened with the outcome they wanted, one live view instead of manual reporting, and only then showed how the data underneath connected. I pitched the depth differently for the **30-plus** system leads than for the two department heads. And I took their technical objections live in the room rather than promising to come back with answers.
>
> They adopted it in that session. It gave back roughly **400 hours a year**.
>
> [Link: tie to {company}'s {relevant need}. Default: start with the buyer's success metric, demo the workflow that moves it, and show only enough architecture to make it believable.]

**Anchors: 20 systems, 30-plus stakeholders, 400 hours returned.**

### Story 3 - Objection handling and stakeholder buy-in

**Evidence status:** `ELIGIBLE`

**Question shapes:** disagreement, pushback, resistance, influence without authority, changing someone's mind, challenging a decision.

> At Apple, headquarters had decided to support a POS migration with one centralised training approach. I thought it would fail, because the stores genuinely ran differently by location. I had no authority over that decision and no interest in arguing about preferences.
>
> So I gathered evidence on how store processes actually varied, translated those differences into the operational risk they would create, and put the case to the Shanghai development team. I did not just object either. I brought an alternative: keep the same migration objective, deliver the training store by store.
>
> They reversed the original plan and moved to localised delivery.
>
> What I took from it is that you handle an objection by respecting the other side's goal, making the hidden constraint visible, and offering a route that still gets them there.
>
> [Link: tie to {company}'s {relevant need}. Default: same approach when customer evidence needs to move Product or Engineering here.]

**Anchors: central plan, store evidence, decision reversed.**

### Story 4 - Commercial judgment and value selling

**Evidence status:** `ELIGIBLE`

**Question shapes:** commercial value, qualification, ROI, expansion, upsell, customer focus, turning a need into revenue.

> At Apple, a Hong Kong broadcaster walked in with no formal enterprise opportunity attached to them. I suspected the request in front of me was a symptom of something bigger, so I started asking how they chose, bought and managed their devices.
>
> I earned the right to keep asking by giving them real hands-on technical support and building them a custom pricing portal, then qualified the operational and commercial requirements over several conversations. When they raised worries about controlling a larger device fleet, I widened the conversation from hardware purchasing to device management instead of treating it as somebody else's problem.
>
> It became a **£50,000** deal over **three months**, and the same approach across a **50-plus** account portfolio brought in **£350,000** of new B2B pipeline.
>
> [Link: tie to {company}'s {relevant need}. Default: I qualify on the business problem, not the shopping list, which is where expansion revenue comes from.]

**Anchors: casual visit, £50,000 deal, £350,000 pipeline.**

### Story 5 - Problem-solving under pressure and fast learning

**Evidence status:** `ELIGIBLE`

**Question shapes:** challenge, deadline, pressure, ambiguity, regulation, learning something new fast, project coordination.

> At Bank of China, the HKMA issued a mandate that meant shipping Geo-IP fraud detection with impossible-travel logic in **14 days**. I had not worked that regulation before, and I owned turning the legal language into something internal teams and a third-party vendor could actually build and prove.
>
> I broke the mandate into functional, data, testing and approval requirements, pinned an owner and a dependency to each one, and held every decision against the evidence the regulator would ask for. That is what stopped the scope growing. Then I got the technical teams and the vendor working to one set of acceptance criteria instead of three.
>
> We shipped inside the deadline and met **100 percent** of the mandates.
>
> [Link: tie to {company}'s {relevant need}. Default: I can make a complicated obligation executable, which is what a customer with a hard deadline is actually buying.]

**Anchors: HKMA, 14 days, 100 percent delivered.**

## Fast story selector

| If the question mentions... | Use | Competency |
|---|---|---|
| Discovery, requirements, ambiguity, customer needs, scoping, tailoring a solution | No eligible story yet; Story 1 is review-required | Consultative discovery and scoping |
| Explaining, presenting, non-technical audience, demo, adoption, complexity | No eligible story yet; Story 2 is review-required | Technical translation |
| Objection, pushback, disagreement, influence, no authority, changing a decision | Story 3 | Objection handling and stakeholder buy-in |
| Commercial value, qualification, ROI, expansion, upsell, sales, customer focus | Story 4 | Commercial judgment and value selling |
| Challenge, pressure, deadline, regulation, learning fast, coordination, failure risk | Story 5 | Problem-solving under pressure and fast learning |
| Teamwork, cross-functional, working with engineering | Story 1 or 3 | Pick by whether the point is scoping or influence |
| Prioritisation, competing deadlines, being interrupted | Supporting point 2 | Process and prioritisation |
| AI, machine learning, responsible AI, analytical ability | Supporting point 1 | AI fluency |

## Supporting proof points

Not master stories. Use these when a question falls outside the five, or when an interviewer pushes for a second example.

1. **AI fluency and responsible AI.** MSc project auditing an AI credit scoring model for fairness and interpretability. Cut high-risk incorrect approvals by **24 cases per 100**, improved the age-demographic fairness score by **0.255**, and generated plain-language SHAP trust reports so non-technical stakeholders could defend the model's decisions. Use for analytical ability, AI governance, explainability, or learning an emerging technology.

2. **Process instinct and automation.** At Huawei, automated tracking across **60 to 80** live sample units with VBA, scheduled reminders and template-generated sign-off, killing a daily manual check and about an hour a week of paperwork. At Apple, removed roughly **1,500 hours** of manual work a year across **six** flagship sites. Use for prioritisation, improving a process, or preventing things being dropped.

3. **Prioritisation under interruption.** At Apple, an unplanned business customer arrived while workshop prep was blocked out. Judged they needed a fast response, not a full answer on the spot. Time-boxed it to 30 minutes, captured the context, agreed a follow-up, then went back to the protected block so neither commitment slipped. Use for the "how do you prioritise" question, which comes up in almost every screen.

**Honest technical read, if pushed:** hands-on at analytics grade. Python and pandas from the MSc, SQL, Grafana, Power BI, VBA. Designs systems and works with the people who build them. Has not owned production code in a professional setting. Say this plainly if asked. Do not inflate it.

## Adapting one story to a different question

Keep the factual middle stable. Change the first sentence and the last.

Story 5, three openings:

- Asked about a **challenge**: "The hardest one was turning a new regulatory mandate into a working solution in 14 days."
- Asked about **pressure**: "The tightest deadline I have worked to was a 14-day regulatory delivery at Bank of China."
- Asked about **coordination**: "A good example of cross-functional coordination is an HKMA-mandated fraud detection change that ran across internal teams and a third-party vendor."

Same actions, same result, different link.

## Rehearsal rule

After any answer, a listener should be able to repeat five things back to you:

1. The problem.
2. What you were responsible for.
3. What you personally did.
4. The number at the end.
5. Why it matters to this company.

If one of those is missing, the answer is not ready. Say it again.
