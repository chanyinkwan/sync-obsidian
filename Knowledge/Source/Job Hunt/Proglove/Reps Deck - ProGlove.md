## Deck B - Five competency stories

### Story 1 - Consultative discovery and scoping

Question shapes: discovery, requirements, ambiguity, customer needs, scoping, tailoring.

> At Huawei, I supported a consumer-device opportunity for Hutchison Three covering multiple European operating companies.
> 
> The initial request looked like a straightforward product-selection exercise, but each participating market had a different combination of product, network, compliance and commercial requirements. Recommending one handset or configuration without validating those differences would have created a significant commitment risk for the pursuit team.
> 
> I therefore treated the selection as a structured presales qualification process.
> 
> First, I coordinated an internal scoping workshop. We decomposed the customer’s requirements into product capabilities, technical compatibility, compliance considerations and commercial needs. I then routed each area to the appropriate product or subject-matter team so that our response was based on confirmed capabilities rather than assumptions.

Next, we translated those requirements into customer use cases and shortlisted the most suitable device options. In the customer-facing session, we validated our understanding of the use cases, explained how the proposed products addressed them and demonstrated the relevant capabilities.

That session also exposed a material gap. The customer expected access to applications normally distributed through Google Play, while the proposed Huawei devices did not provide the same native Google Play experience. Instead of allowing that dependency to remain hidden, I coordinated the relevant specialists and arranged a focused follow-up demonstration of an alternative app-access approach. We presented it with its limitations and dependencies rather than positioning it as a fully equivalent replacement.

The result was a more defensible product recommendation and a clearer scope for the pursuit team. We could distinguish what the devices supported, what still required customer validation and where third-party dependencies existed before making a final commercial commitment.

What makes this a presales story is not simply that I selected a product. I took the customer from fragmented requirements through qualification, capability mapping, demonstration, gap identification and risk-managed recommendation.

I would apply the same approach to a ProGlove opportunity. I would first qualify the warehouse workflows, barcode types, connectivity, WMS integration and user requirements; establish the operational baseline; select the appropriate device and software configuration; and then agree measurable PoC acceptance criteria. That ensures the pilot validates a customer outcome rather than merely demonstrating a scanner.

**Anchors: three workshops, six subnets, validated scope.**

### Story 2 - Technical translation

Question shapes: explaining something complex, non-technical audience, presentation, demo, adoption.

> At Bank of China, teams were losing about **eight hours a week** assembling reports by hand across **20 systems**. I replaced that with a single Grafana observability view. Building it was the easy part. It was only worth anything if the system owners and the department heads actually used it.
>
> So in the demo I opened with the outcome they wanted, one live view instead of manual reporting, and only then showed how the data underneath connected. I pitched the depth differently for the 12 system leads than for the two department heads. And I took their technical objections live in the room rather than promising to come back with answers.
>
> They adopted it in that session. It gave back roughly **400 hours a year**.
>
> Link: Same shape as an INSIGHT conversation. Open with the throughput number the ops director cares about, show the floor team the workflow, and only enough of the platform to make it believable.

**Anchors: 20 systems, 30-plus stakeholders, 400 hours returned.**

### Story 3 - Objection handling and stakeholder buy-in

Question shapes: disagreement, pushback, influence without authority, changing a decision.

> At Apple, headquarters had decided to support a POS migration with one centralised training approach. I thought it would fail, because the stores genuinely ran differently by location. I had no authority over that decision and no interest in arguing about preferences.
>
> So I gathered evidence on how store processes actually varied, translated those differences into the operational risk they would create, and put the case to the Shanghai development team. I did not just object either. I brought an alternative: keep the same migration objective, deliver the training store by store.
>
> They reversed the original plan and moved to localised delivery.
>
> What I took from it is that you handle an objection by respecting the other side's goal, making the hidden constraint visible, and offering a route that still gets them there.
>
> Link: Same approach when a customer's floor evidence needs to move Product or Engineering in Munich. Respect their goal, make the constraint visible, bring an alternative route.

**Anchors: central plan, store evidence, decision reversed.**

### Story 4 - Commercial judgment and value selling

Question shapes: commercial value, qualification, ROI, expansion, upsell, customer focus.

> At Apple, a Hong Kong broadcaster walked in with no formal enterprise opportunity attached to them. I suspected the request in front of me was a symptom of something bigger, so I started asking how they chose, bought and managed their devices.
>
> I earned the right to keep asking by giving them real hands-on technical support and building them a custom pricing portal, then qualified the operational and commercial requirements over several conversations. When they raised worries about controlling a larger device fleet, I widened the conversation from hardware purchasing to device management instead of treating it as somebody else's problem.
>
> It became a **£50,000** deal over **three months**, and the same approach across a **50-plus** account portfolio brought in **£350,000** of new B2B pipeline.
>
> Link: Hardware plus fleet software is exactly ProGlove's shape. A scanner conversation that becomes an INSIGHT subscription is the same widening I did from device purchasing to device management.

**Anchors: casual visit, £50,000 deal, £350,000 pipeline.**

### Story 5 - Problem-solving under pressure and fast learning

Question shapes: challenge, deadline, pressure, regulation, learning fast, coordination.

> At Bank of China, the HKMA issued a mandate that meant shipping Geo-IP fraud detection with impossible-travel logic in **14 days**. I had not worked that regulation before, and I owned turning the legal language into something internal teams and a third-party vendor could actually build and prove.
>
> I broke the mandate into functional, data, testing and approval requirements, pinned an owner and a dependency to each one, and held every decision against the evidence the regulator would ask for. That is what stopped the scope growing. Then I got the technical teams and the vendor working to one set of acceptance criteria instead of three.
>
> We shipped inside the deadline and met **100 percent** of the mandates.
>
> Link: A customer with a peak-season go-live is buying exactly this. Someone who can turn a hard constraint into an executable rollout plan with owners and acceptance criteria.

**Anchors: HKMA, 14 days, 100 percent delivered.**
