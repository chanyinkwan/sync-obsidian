## 3. How my day to day actually looks, per role

Written to be spoken. Read these out loud once before Thursday.

### Huawei, now

My week runs on two clocks. China HQ is eight hours ahead, so my morning is their afternoon, and that window is where all the real decisions get made. I start the day clearing what came in overnight on WeLink, which is usually a product line asking for a number, or a roadmap update that changes something I already told a customer.

Then the day splits three ways. First is portfolio work. I sit between HQ product lines and a pan-European operator group across five markets, and my job is to turn a China roadmap into something a European operator will actually buy. That means reading what HQ shipped, working out what the local market cares about, and writing the version that survives contact with a customer.

Second is the commercial side. Bids and pricing. When a group-level tender lands, I pull the pieces together from people who do not report to me, the account managers who own the client relationship, the channel GTM lead who owns the marketplace pipeline, and the technical architect who owns the solution. I hold the deadline and the story. Nobody made me the owner, I just became the person who knows where everything is.

Third is the language layer, which is invisible on a CV and is most of the value. Everything I produce exists twice, in Mandarin for HQ and in English for the region, and the two versions have to say the same thing. I have watched deals slow down because a requirement got softened in translation.

What I protect: roughly a fifth of the day stays blank on purpose, because the reactive stuff will fill any space you give it.

**The PM translation:** I already run the loop the JD describes. HQ roadmap in, regional strategy out, bid and pricing execution in the middle, and a cross-functional team I influence rather than command.

### Bank of China, Product Analyst

This was the most product-shaped job I have had. I owned features on BOC Pay and the e-banking platform, and the day started with whatever the regulator, the vendor, or production had done to us overnight.

A typical morning was requirements. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority publishes a mandate in legal language, and somebody has to turn it into something engineering can build. That was me. I would read the regulation, write the implementation plan, then sit with the fraud team to work out what Impossible Travel actually means when an account logs in in two cities in hours apart.

Afternoons were delivery and people. I ran a three-person analyst team through UAT, and because BOC Pay launched into four regions we could not test in one time zone. I designed a follow-the-sun cycle so testing handed over every eight hours instead of stopping overnight. That is where the fifteen man-days came back.

The rest of the week was stakeholder work I volunteered for. Fifty-plus teams were writing the same status reports by hand every week, so I built a Grafana layer that killed the manual reporting, then spent weeks demoing it to system leads and department heads until they trusted it. The demo was the product. If the room does not adopt it, it does not exist.

**The PM translation:** regulated delivery on a hard deadline, multi-region launch, and adoption driven by demo rather than mandate.

### Apple, Operations Strategy Analyst

Six flagship stores, and my job was to find the thing that was quietly costing everyone hours and then remove it.

Mornings were on the floor. I would watch how the store actually ran, not how the playbook said it ran, because the gap between those two is where every good project came from. The eight-hour weekly productivity drain that turned into the ticketing system, I found that by standing in a back office watching people chase each other.

Middays were build time. I wrote the reporting architecture, the ticketing tool, the playbooks. Nothing fancy, but it had to survive without me, because I was not going to be there forever and a tool that needs its author is not a tool.

Afternoons were the political half. The Shanghai development team ran demo training centrally, one format for every store, and it did not fit. I could not order them to change it, so I collected evidence that operations genuinely varied by location and presented it back until they moved to store-specific delivery. That took several rounds. Then during the EasyPay POS migration I ran ten-plus Floor Champions, each with a written execution playbook, so the migration never touched customer experience. Uptime held and NPS stayed at 92.

**The PM translation:** discovery by observation, build, then adoption by evidence with an APAC HQ team who outranked me.

### Apple, Business Development, Corporate Solutions

The most commercial of the four. A fifty-plus account B2B portfolio, and the team's default was reactive. Somebody churns, then we notice.

My day was half account-facing and half analysis. I would take the account data, build diagnostic models against it, and work backwards from missed revenue to cause. Twelve-plus distinct causes came out, and once you can name the cause you can act before the churn instead of after it. That is the shift I am proudest of, from tracking to retention.

The other half was operational. Two hundred-plus people, peak-time scheduling that collided every week, five hours of manual floor planning. I built a capacity planning tool that mapped staffing against actual customer traffic and the conflicts went away.

**The PM translation:** commercial ownership of a portfolio, root-cause analysis on revenue, and the instinct to build the tool rather than file the complaint.
